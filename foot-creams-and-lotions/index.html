Cutaneous Pharmacology: Keratolytic Science & Epidermal Barrier Repair

The skin covering the human heel is structurally unlike any other surface on the body. It contains zero sebaceous glands, meaning it cannot produce natural sebum to maintain surface lubrication and suppleness. Instead, it relies entirely on eccrine perspiration and intracellular lipid bilayers (composed of a precise stoichiometric balance of ceramides, cholesterol, and free fatty acids) to prevent transepidermal water loss (TEWL) and preserve cutaneous elasticity.

Under daily ambulatory weight-bearing, vertical impact forces flatten the subcutaneous calcaneal fat pad, stretching the overlying stratum corneum laterally. If the skin is dehydrated (calcaneal xerosis), it loses its tensile compliance. Rather than flexing, the hardened stratum corneum fractures along mechanical stress lines, producing deep, agonizing fissures that can bleed and become chronically infected. Standard cosmetic body lotions containing water, mineral oil, or low-grade glycerin cannot penetrate this hardened protein barrier.

Medical resolution requires topical keratolytics, predominantly medical-grade urea (carbamide). At therapeutic concentrations between 20% and 40%, urea alters the hydrogen bonds of keratin proteins, drawing water into compacted corneocytes and activating natural proteolytic enzymes that dissolve desmosomal cell bridges. When combined with lipid-soluble salicylic acid (BHA) to unclog pores or lactic acid (AHA) to stimulate endogenous ceramide synthesis, high-potency foot creams restore normal epidermal shedding and close painful fissures within 7 to 14 days of nightly occlusion therapy.
