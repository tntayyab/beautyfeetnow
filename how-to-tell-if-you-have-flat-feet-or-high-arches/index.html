How to Tell if You Have Flat Feet or High Arches: 4 Tests (2026)

Medically Reviewed by Elena Rostova, DPM (Board-Certified Podiatrist) • Updated November 4, 2026

Direct Answer & Medical Summary: To determine your foot arch type accurately at home, perform the clinical Wet Paper Footprint Test: wet the bottom of your bare foot and step firmly onto a sheet of heavy cardboard or paper grocery bag. If the print shows almost the entire outline of your sole with no inward curve, you have flat feet (pes planus). If the print shows a normal moderate curve through the midfoot connecting heel and ball, you have neutral arches. If only a razor-thin strip or a complete gap separates your heel and ball print, you have high arches (pes cavus).

Key Clinical Takeaways & Rules
  • The Wet Paper Footprint Test is the gold-standard at-home diagnostic for evaluating dynamic static arch morphology.
  • Outsole wear patterns reveal dynamic gait: medial heel and big toe erosion signals overpronation (flat feet); lateral outer tread wear signals underpronation (high arches).
  • Flat feet collapse the medial longitudinal arch, stretching the plantar fascia and predisposing to bunions, shin splints, and pinch calluses.
  • High arches concentrate 100% of body weight onto the heel and ball of the foot, predisposing to metatarsalgia and plantar fasciitis.
  • Arch type dictates insole selection: flat feet require semi-rigid motion control orthotics; high arches require shock-absorbing soft cushioned insoles.

1. Functional Anatomy: The Three Arches of the Human Foot

Every step you take subjects your feet to complex mechanical forces. Your foot is not a single rigid block; it is an architectural marvel composed of 26 bones, 33 joints, and over 100 muscles, tendons, and ligaments configured into three distinct dynamic arches: the Medial Longitudinal Arch (the primary arch along the inside of your foot), the Lateral Longitudinal Arch (along the outside edge), and the Transverse Metatarsal Arch (spanning across the ball of the foot).

Together, these three arches form a dynamic vault that performs two opposing mechanical functions during every gait cycle:

  • Mobile Shock Absorber (Midstance): At heel strike, the subtalar joint pronates, allowing the arches to slightly flatten and attenuate kinetic ground reaction forces.
  • Rigid Propulsion Lever (Toe-Off): As you push off your toes, the windlass mechanism tightens, elevating the arch and locking the tarsal bones into a rigid lever to propel you forward.

When this delicate architectural balance fails—either by collapsing into pes planus (flat feet) or locking into pes cavus (rigid high arches)—the entire kinetic chain suffers. Diagnosing your specific arch morphology is the mandatory first step before purchasing running shoes or selecting orthotic insoles.

2. Clinical Test #1: The Wet Paper Footprint Test (Step-by-Step)

The Wet Paper Footprint Test is the most famous, validated self-assessment tool in podiatry. It provides an immediate visual map of your foot’s dynamic contact area under body weight. Follow these steps for 100% diagnostic accuracy:

  1. Prepare Your Surface: Place a flat, heavy brown paper grocery bag, a sheet of dark construction paper, or a piece of cardboard on a level, hard floor.
  2. Wet the Plantar Sole: Step into a shallow pan of water to thoroughly wet the bottom of your bare foot. Shake off excess drips so the skin is moist, not dripping.
  3. Step Firmly: Step naturally onto the paper with your full body weight, standing upright for 2 to 3 seconds. Step off without dragging your foot.
  4. Analyze the Imprint:
    • Flat Foot (Pes Planus): The imprint shows almost the entire outline of your foot. There is no inward curve along the arch, and the midfoot is as wide as the ball and heel.
    • Neutral Arch (Normal): The imprint displays a distinct, healthy inward curve through the midfoot, filling roughly half of the arch area.
    • High Arch (Pes Cavus): The imprint reveals only the heel and ball of the foot, connected by a razor-thin outer strip—or disconnected completely with zero midfoot contact.

3. Clinical Test #2: Shoe Outsole Wear Diagnostics

Your everyday walking shoes or running sneakers are a permanent historical record of your gait kinetics. Turn an old pair of well-worn shoes upside down and inspect the rubber outsole tread:

  • Overpronation (Flat Feet Wear Pattern): Heavy, premature rubber erosion localized along the inside (medial) edge of the heel and directly beneath the big toe (first metatarsal head). This wear pattern indicates that your foot rolls excessively inward during push-off.
  • Underpronation / Supination (High Arch Wear Pattern): Heavy wear localized entirely along the outside (lateral) edge of the entire shoe sole. This indicates that your rigid arch fails to pronate, concentrating all kinetic friction on the outer foot margin.
  • Neutral Wear Pattern: Symmetrical wear in an S-shaped pattern from the lateral heel strike across to the center of the ball.

Compare this kinetic pattern with your skin health; as explained in our guide on why foot calluses return, medial heel wear correlates directly with painful pinch calluses.

4. Clinical Test #3: The Navicular Drop & Finger Slide Test

To differentiate between a flexible flat foot and a rigid structural flat foot, clinical podiatrists perform the Navicular Drop Test:

Sit in a chair with your feet flat on the floor (unweighted). Locate the navicular tuberosity—the prominent bony bump on the inside of your midfoot roughly 1 inch in front of your ankle bone. Place a credit card vertically against the floor next to this bone and mark its seated height with a pen. Now stand up, placing full body weight on your feet. If the bone drops downward by more than 10 millimeters, you have hypermobile, flexible flat feet with ligamentous laxity (Journal of Orthopaedic & Sports Physical Therapy).

For high arches, perform the Finger Slide Test: while standing barefoot on a hard floor with full body weight, attempt to slide your index finger under your arch. If you can easily slide two or three fingers under the arch without touching the skin, you have rigid pes cavus.

5. Flat Feet (Pes Planus): Biomechanical Consequences & Risks

Collapsed flat feet trigger an upstream chain reaction of kinetic dysfunctions:

  • Plantar Fasciitis: As the arch flattens, the plantar fascia is stretched beyond its physiological elasticity, leading to chronic enthesopathy and heel spurs. Review our comparison of rigid vs soft orthotics for plantar fasciitis.
  • Posterior Tibial Tendon Dysfunction (PTTD): The tendon responsible for supporting the arch undergoes chronic strain, leading to adult-acquired flatfoot deformity.
  • Patellofemoral Knee Pain: Internal tibial rotation forces the patella to track laterally in its groove, causing runner's knee.
  • Tarsal Tunnel Nerve Compression: Inward arch collapse stretches the posterior tibial nerve, causing burning night pain; read our diagnostic guide on why feet burn at night and clinical relief.

6. High Arches (Pes Cavus): Biomechanical Consequences & Risks

While flat feet suffer from excessive twisting, high arches suffer from an almost total inability to attenuate impact shock:

  • Severe Metatarsalgia: 80% of ground force is concentrated directly on the ball of the foot, causing intense burning pain and intractable plantar keratomas (calluses).
  • Heel Fat Pad Atrophy: Without arch shock absorption, the calcaneal fat pad is crushed against the heel bone, leading to chronic stone bruises.
  • Tibial Stress Fractures: Shockwaves travel directly up the rigid skeletal column into the shin bones and hip joints.
  • Peroneal Tendonitis & Ankle Sprains: Walking on the outer edge of the foot stretches the lateral ankle ligaments, predisposing to recurrent inversion ankle sprains.

8. The Corrective Insole Matching Guide: Flat Feet vs High Arches

Your arch type dictates your insole prescription:

  • If You Have Flat Feet: You need a semi-rigid motion control orthotic with a firm polypropylene arch bridge, a deep heel cup (18–20 mm), and a medial rearfoot post. This physically prevents inward pronation. Explore our verified rankings in the best orthotic insoles for flat feet.

Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

Can flat feet cause knee, hip, and lower back pain?

Yes, absolutely. The human kinetic chain functions as a unified biomechanical system. When your foot arch collapses inward during overpronation, the talus bone rotates medially, forcing the tibia (shin bone) to internally rotate. This internal rotation forces the knee into a valgus (knock-kneed) position and tilts the pelvis anteriorly, straining the lumbar spine and triggering chronic lower back and hip pain.

Is having flat feet always bad or painful?

Not necessarily. Many individuals possess asymptomatic, flexible flat feet and experience zero pain or functional limitation throughout their lives (often seen in professional sprinters and athletes). However, if your flat feet cause foot fatigue, medial arch aching, bunions, or plantar fasciitis, biomechanical intervention with supportive orthotics is clinically necessary.

Can you fix flat feet with exercises?

In adults, exercises cannot permanently raise the bony structural height of a collapsed skeletal arch, because the adult tarsal bones and plantar ligaments are permanently set. However, targeted physical therapy exercises (such as calf stretches, toe scrunches, and eccentric posterior tibial heel drops) can significantly strengthen the dynamic stabilizing muscles, reducing fatigue and preventing progressive deformity.

Why do people with high arches get more calluses on the ball of their foot?

People with rigid high arches lack the normal shock-absorbing flexibility of a flattening arch. During walking, ground reaction forces cannot be dispersed across the midfoot, concentrating up to 80% of total body weight onto the second and third metatarsal heads. The skin responds to this vertical impact pressure through mechanotransduction, building dense calluses (intractable plantar keratomas) as protective armor.

What type of shoes are best for flat feet vs high arches?

For flat feet: Choose 'motion control' or 'stability' shoes with a firm medial post, a rigid heel counter, and a semi-rigid arch support insole. For high arches: Choose 'neutral cushioned' shoes with abundant midsole EVA/PU cushioning, a flexible outsole, and an insole with a deep heel cup to absorb shock.
